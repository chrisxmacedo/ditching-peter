Adapting for AI

Prompting AI
with ARA.

AI assistants are powerful — but they fail in the same three ways that employees do. They overstep when authority is unclear. They produce the wrong thing when responsibility is undefined. And they underperform when the skill you need — a specific framework, tool, or method — is never named, so they fall back on a generic one. The fix is the same: define all three before you start.

The ARA prompt template

Master Prompt — copy and adapt
You are acting as [role title] within [organisation or team context]. Authority — decisions you can make without checking: - [specific decision + limit, e.g. "Approve copy changes that do not alter pricing or product claims"] - [specific decision + limit] - Anything outside these limits: flag it and ask before proceeding. Responsibility — what you must produce: - [deliverable + recipient, e.g. "A final draft delivered to me, ready to publish"] - [deliverable + recipient] - Format: [specify format, length, tone] Ability — the named skills, tools, or frameworks to use: - [named skill or framework, e.g. "Direct-response copywriting (PAS framework)"] - [named software, e.g. "Jira, Excel, or the Microsoft Word suite"] - [named methodology, e.g. "Lean Six Sigma (DMAIC) analysis"] Now: [the actual task — put the situational facts and context here, not under Ability]
Why this works: Authority stops the AI guessing what it should decide vs. flag. Responsibility pins the output so it doesn't wander. Ability names the actual skills, tools, and frameworks for the job — a copywriting framework, Excel, Jira, a named methodology — so the AI works to a known standard instead of inventing a generic one.

You are acting as a Senior Copywriter for a B2B SaaS company selling expense management software to finance teams at companies with 50–500 employees. Authority: - Adjust headline and body copy freely - You may reorder sections for flow - Do not change pricing, feature names, or CTAs without flagging Responsibility: - Deliver a revised landing page hero section (headline + subheadline + 3 bullet points) - Tone: direct, no fluff, confident but not aggressive - Length: headline under 10 words, subheadline under 25 words Ability: - Direct-response copywriting using the PAS (Problem–Agitate–Solution) framework - Copy editing to a brand tone-of-voice guide (cutting filler like "seamless", "powerful", "robust") - Drafting in Google Docs Now: Rewrite the hero section (current copy below). Context: the customer's main pain is a 3-day month-end close caused by missing receipts; our differentiator is auto-reconciliation against bank feeds.
What the AI now knows to do
AuthCan rewrite copy freely — but will flag if asked to change pricing or CTA
RespProduces: hero headline + subheadline + 3 bullets, within specified length
RespTone and format are explicit — no guessing what "good" looks like
AbilityPAS-framework copywriting — a proven persuasion structure, not generic prose
AbilityTone-of-voice editing in Google Docs — filler cut, length limit held

The three failure modes

Each maps to one missing pillar — the same failures you see in an under-defined role.

01

Undefined authority produces scope creep

An AI without authority boundaries will make decisions you didn't intend to delegate. It will rewrite the CTA, change the product name, alter the tone entirely. Every decision the AI makes that you didn't explicitly grant is a decision made without authority.

02

Undefined responsibility produces drift

Without a clear deliverable — format, length, recipient, purpose — the AI produces something technically responsive but practically useless. It answered the question you typed, not the question you needed answered. Responsibility pins the output.

03

Undefined ability produces generic craft

Ability is the named skill, tool, or framework the work demands — the PAS copywriting framework, Lean Six Sigma, Excel, Jira. Specify it and the AI works to that standard. Leave it blank and it defaults to a generic version — a first draft you have to rewrite entirely.
